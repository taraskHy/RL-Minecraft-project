From falling off the first block to the goal

The untrained network and the first two snapshots fall off at once. After 10 and 20 updates it gets two and five platforms in, then makes no more progress: at 10 it stands still, and at 20 it hops on the spot. From 35 updates on, every snapshot reaches the goal, and later ones are faster: 181 ticks at 35 updates, 156 at 400.

Training

Main run: 1.57 billion game ticks in 2.6 hours

16,384 games run at once on one GPU, at about 170,000 game ticks per second including the network updates. Every 50 updates the policy plays 1,200 held-out courses, 200 per difficulty level.

The curve is the first main run, with discount 0.99. The final policy uses discount 0.995, the only setting in our hyperparameter study that beat the defaults by more than 3 points on validation, in all three seeds. We trained it at full length, 3,000 updates and then 1,000 with obstacles, and chose its checkpoint on the simulator's validation courses: 97.6% there, against 96.6% for the first final policy.

Reward design

The fall penalty matters most

We removed one reward term at a time and trained each variant with 3 seeds. Without the fall penalty, the hardest level drops to 54 to 57% in every seed. Without the distance-to-goal reward, the final score is the same but learning takes about 3 times as many ticks to reach 80%.

Hiding the platform positions, so the agent sees only nearby blocks and the goal direction, costs the most: 43% on level 5.

How the agent learned neos

Trained on neo courses with PPO alone, the agent made none of the 13 neo jump types. On 11 of them it stood still until the 10-second stall limit, every time. Standing still cost less reward than a likely fall. A stall penalty made refusing count as a failure, but random exploration almost never finds the curved run a neo needs.

So we gave it examples. A search in the bot's own action space found 50,706 landing sequences, each checked in the environment, and the agent imitated 12,800 of them while PPO kept training. Three rounds of DAgger followed: the agent plays, the search looks for a landing from each neo takeoff it reaches, and the moves it finds become new examples. The search found a landing from 84% and 88% of those takeoffs in rounds 2 and 3, and from 93% in round 1, where a detection bug also counted one-block-up jumps as takeoffs: on that round's courses only 43% of the detected takeoffs were real neos.

The bot finds its own route

After every landing, a simple planner groups the blocks within 12 blocks into platforms, leaves out walls, pillars and head-hitter beams, and picks the next three: easy jumps toward the goal. The trained policy jumps to them, unchanged. Only the goal's position is given.

The first two columns use the final policy; with the first final policy the planner made 89.6% and 86.2% in the simulator. Most planner failures follow a shortcut: a later platform is in reach, so it skips ahead, and that jump is harder than the course's own next one. The learned column is a model trained to find platforms itself from a wide block scan and a memory of its last landings. It is weaker on the 1,200-course sets, and obstacles fool it: 10 of its 23 real-game failures with obstacles end standing on a beam or a pillar.
