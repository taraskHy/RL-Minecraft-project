Reinforcement learning course project · October 2026

Learning Minecraft Parkour

A PPO agent trained in a GPU copy of Minecraft's movement physics completes parkour courses it has never seen, and does the same in the real game. It also learned neo jumps from demonstrations, runs on ice, can find its own route, and three specialists are now distilled into one network that plays every skill.

Watch it learn

From falling off the first block to the goal

The untrained network and the first two snapshots fall off at once. After 10 and 20 updates it gets two and five platforms in, then makes no more progress: at 10 it stands still, and at 20 it hops on the spot. From 35 updates on, every snapshot reaches the goal, and later ones are faster: 181 ticks at 35 updates, 156 at 400.

Training

Main run: 1.57 billion game ticks in 2.6 hours

16,384 games run at once on one GPU, at about 170,000 game ticks per second including the network updates. Every 50 updates the policy plays 1,200 held-out courses, 200 per difficulty level.

all levelslevel 5, the hardest

Success on held-out courses of the first main run (discount 0.99), first evaluated after 50 updates. The dip at 1.0 B ticks was two bad updates in a row while the learning rate sat at its cap; it recovered by the next evaluation.

One hyperparameter made the final policy

We changed one setting at a time around the defaults and trained each for 0.52 B ticks. Only a longer horizon, discount 0.995 instead of 0.99, beat the defaults by more than 3 points on validation, in all three seeds. We retrained the final policy with it at full length: 3,000 updates, then 1,000 with obstacles.

It completes 98.4% of the standard test courses and 95.2% with obstacles, against 97.1% and 93.6% for the first final policy. Level 5, the hardest, rose from 90.5% to 93.5%.

Reward design

The fall penalty matters most

We removed one reward term at a time and trained each variant with 3 seeds. Without the fall penalty, the hardest level drops to 54 to 57% in every seed. Without the distance-to-goal reward, the final score is the same but learning takes about 3 times as many ticks to reach 80%.

Hiding the platform positions, so the agent sees only nearby blocks and the goal direction, costs the most: 43% on level 5.

Harder jumps

Neo jumps: learned from demonstrations

  • 16/18possible Single neo layouts (jumping around a wall) that a tick-by-tick search could land. Only 1 in 2,000 to 1 in 16,000 sampled move sequences works.
  • 13/13learned Single neo types of levels 6 and 7, the possible layouts that a scripted strategy also lands. The agent lands all 13 after learning from demonstrations, up from 0 with PPO alone. From a standing start the chosen neo policy lands 60.6% of tries on average, over 16 start angles in the simulator.
  • 0/64none found Double and triple neo layouts, 36 from standing and 28 with a 3 to 4 block run-up. The same search landed none of them, so we treat them as impossible.

How the agent learned neos

Trained on neo courses with PPO alone, the agent made none of the 13 neo jump types. On 11 of them it stood still until the 10-second stall limit, every time: standing still cost less reward than a likely fall. A stall penalty made refusing count as a failure, but random exploration almost never finds the curved run a neo needs.

So we gave it examples. A search in the bot's own action space found 50,706 landing sequences, each checked in the environment, and the agent imitated 12,800 of them while PPO kept training. Three rounds of DAgger followed: the agent plays, the search looks for a landing from each neo takeoff it reaches, and the moves it finds become new examples.

Planning the hard jumps

A second route keeps the final policy and adds a search: when the bot stands on a planned takeoff, a cross-entropy search with the exact physics finds a move sequence from its exact state, and the bot plays it. With every jump planned, braking and an approach phase, it completes 72.0% of 218 validation neo courses in the simulator (the policy alone: 0%). With every jump planned and braking, the offline bot completes 25 of the 31 neo courses, the same courses as the simulator.

New ground

Ice, head hitters, shaped blocks, slime and ladders

Ice: nearly free

The final policy was never trained on ice, yet it completes 95.6% of the standard test courses with every platform made of ice, against 98.4% on stone; the loss is mostly on level 5 (86.0% against 93.5%). On a new validation set with ice on 30% of the platforms it completes 97.5% (600 courses).

We still trained 20 seeds with ice on 30% of the platforms. On the ordinary validation courses they score 96.4 to 97.8%, around the final policy's 97.6%, and the best ice specialist reaches 97.8% on the ice set. All numbers are from the simulator.

Five new block kinds

We added head hitters, ice run-ups, shaped blocks (slabs, stairs, posts), slime bounces and ladder climbs to the physics; each matches prismarine-physics to 1.4e-14 blocks or better over 60,000 ticks. Alone, the final policy makes 99.1% of the shaped-block jumps, 68.2% of head hitters, 48.9% of ice run-ups, 39.9% of slime bounces and 37.5% of ladder climbs.

Trained on them for 1,000 updates, the specialist g995-extra completes 91.2% of 2,400 test courses over all 12 levels and 89.3% with obstacles. On the six new levels' validation courses it makes 86.1%, where the final policy makes 19.8%.

One network for every skill

Three specialists, distilled into one student

Each skill above has its own network. To get one network that does everything, a student trains with PPO on courses that mix every skill, plus a term that pulls its action choices towards the right teacher on each course. A control run trains the same student with PPO alone. We first measured every candidate on the validation courses of each skill group, and picked the teachers and the starting point by rules fixed before the runs.

On the test courses, one network matches the specialists on the hard skills

Played once on the test courses, the distilled student completes 66.8% of the neo, momentum and wide courses and 86.4% of the new-kind ones; the best specialist on each makes 63.9% (the neo policy) and 85.3% (g995-extra). Those margins are about 1.5 and 1.1 standard errors of one test, so we read them as a match, not a win. On basic and ice courses it is 1.1 and 1.0 points behind the final policy. Without the teachers, PPO alone does as well on the new kinds but loses 9.6 points on neo, momentum and wide jumps.

No course list

The bot finds its own route

After every landing, a simple planner groups the blocks within 12 blocks into platforms, leaves out walls, pillars and head-hitter beams, and picks the next three: short jumps toward the goal. The trained policy jumps to them, unchanged. Only the goal's position is given.

The planner's settings were tuned on validation courses. Most of its failures are route choices, not jumps: it skips ahead to a platform that is in reach but harder to land. The learned column is a model trained to find platforms itself from a wide block scan and a memory of its last landings; decoy pillars fool it.
